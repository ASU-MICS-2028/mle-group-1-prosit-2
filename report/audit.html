Part 5. Model Card

Model Card: NkwaPay default model

Following Mitchell et al. (2019). The evidence is in notebooks/04_transfer_and_fairness.ipynb Part 2 and learnings/tables/11_*.csv. This is a student project, not a licensed product.

1. Model details

  • Built by: MLE Group 1 (Alice, Germain, Joseph, Liza), ICS553, Ashesi University, for the fictional lender NkwaPay.
  • Date and version: November 2026, v1.
  • Type: logistic regression with Platt calibration, fitted on out-of-fold scores from 5 customer-grouped folds (ADR 0016, 0017).
  • Clues:
  • Kenya (10): loan_type, duration, New_versus_Repeat, n_prev_loans, n_finished, n_defaults, past_default_rate, no_known_history, mean_prev_amount, last_amount.
  • Ghana: the model doesn't decide anything yet. A rule by loan type does, using group default rates measured from Zindi's scores (ADR 0021).
  • Fairness choices: none inside the training. Three "borrow often" clues were removed, and first-time applicants above the cut-off get a starter loan (ADR 0020).
  • Companion model: LightGBM on log loan amount, for limits (ADR 0018). Not covered in the tables below.
  • Licence and contact: course work; see the repository.

2. Intended use

  • Primary use: estimate the chance that a short-term mobile-money loan defaults, to support an approve / starter loan / decline decision with up to 3 plain reasons.
  • Primary users: NkwaPay's credit team, and borrowers through the reasons they receive.
  • Out of scope:
  • automatic decisions in Ghana before about 5,000 labelled Ghanaian loans exist;
  • other countries, products, or business lending;
  • setting prices;
  • affordability checks: there is no income data;
  • detecting loans from other lenders: there is no bureau data.

4. Metrics

  • Measures: PR-AUC and lift (suited to about 2% defaults), ROC-AUC, F1, approval rate, good payers wrongly declined, defaulters caught, PPV and NPV, predicted vs actual default, cost per loan.
  • Cut-offs: decline above 2.1% in Kenya and 3.2% in Ghana. These come from the cost of each mistake: 4,700 vs 100 KES, and 2,716 vs 90 GHS (ADR 0007).
  • Uncertainty: customer-bootstrap 95% ranges.
  • Fairness priority: sufficiency (an honest score) first, then good payers wrongly declined. Approval rates are reported but not forced equal, because groups' real default rates differ.

5. Evaluation data

  • Future Kenyan loans: 54,463 loans, 988 defaults, 5,718 customers. Learn from older loans, score the next block.
  • Zindi test applicants: 15,069 Kenyan and 3,525 Ghanaian loans; decisions only, as outcomes aren't available to us.
  • Preparation: shared code in src/features.py; every step is fitted inside the training part.

8. Ethical considerations

  • Sensitive data: financial records. There are no names or ID numbers beyond anonymous customer IDs. Nothing at row level leaves data/.
  • Effect on people: a decision affects access to credit, and a loan can cause over-indebtedness.
  • Harms found and what we did:
  • first-time borrowers shut out completely → starter loan;
  • the model rewarded frequent borrowing → clues removed.
  • Harms still open:
  • a starter loan can still push someone into debt;
  • the limit model offers small borrowers up to 2.8× their usual loan;
  • in Ghana, Type_3 borrowers aren't screened individually, and half of first-time applicants are refused because they ask for Type_2;
  • the Ghana pilot loses about 10,000 GHS per 1,000 applicants unless good customers come back;
  • the data covers only people lenders chose to lend to.
  • Risky uses: using this model to decline Ghanaians at scale, or to chase repeat borrowing.

9. Caveats and recommendations

  • Barely validated on Ghana. Group-level evidence from Zindi's scores puts Ghana's default rate at about 13% and Type_3's at 7%, above the 3.2% break-even (learnings/11 §10). The Ghana rule (ADR 0021) passes 4 of 6 stress tests: it's the best of 729 policies tested, not proven. Trust gates are in ADR 0019: about 5,000 labelled loans, recalibration, monthly drift checks, and a capped pilot.
  • Scores drift. Default rates moved from about 1% (2022) to 9–12% (2023–24). Recalibrate often.
  • The starter loan is costed, not tested. Its value rests on repeat business measured at about 200 KES, from Kenyan lending with no limit model. It breaks even at about 50–130 KES. Run it as a controlled pilot, and track outcomes.
  • Repeat the audit with gender if NkwaPay collects it for bureau reporting.
  • The law asks for things this model can't do alone: a bureau check, an affordability view, and appeal and record-keeping systems. See the compliance map.

Part 6. Datasheet

Datasheet: the data behind the NkwaPay models

Following Gebru et al. (2021). We didn't create these datasets, so many answers are "unknown". Those unknowns are findings in their own right. Counts come from docs/01_data.md.

Part 1: Zindi African Credit Scoring Challenge

Motivation

  • Why was it made? For a machine-learning competition on predicting loan default. We use it as NkwaPay's stand-in loan book.
  • Who made and funded it? Hosted by Zindi. The competition terms call the data "private property of the client". The client's identity is unknown to us.

Composition

  • What is one row? One loan and lender pair. A loan shared between lenders has several rows (920 loan IDs appear in both files).
  • How many? Train: 68,654 rows, 1,258 defaults (1.83%), Kenya only. After removing 190 loans not yet due: 68,464 loans, 6,508 customers. Test: 18,594 rows, no labels: 15,069 Kenyan and 3,525 Ghanaian (446 customers).
  • Representative? Unknown, and probably not. Sampling isn't described. 92% of loans were sent in Jul–Dec 2022, and it holds only people lenders chose to lend to.
  • Label: target, 1 = defaulted. The exact definition (days late, recoveries) is not documented.
  • Missing: no blank cells in the raw files. In practice, no Ghanaian has a known repayment outcome.
  • Known errors or traps:
  • Total_Amount_to_Repay and Lender_portion_to_be_repaid include post-default penalties, so they leak the answer (PR-AUC 0.20 → 0.91);
  • currency isn't stated: we assume local currency;
  • the brief's ProductCategory and IsDefaulted don't exist in the data.
  • Subpopulations: country, loan type, lender, new or repeat, loan size. No gender, age, income or location.
  • Could people be re-identified? Customer IDs are anonymous, but repeated dates and amounts make a loan history fairly distinctive. So we treat rows as personal financial data.
  • Sensitive? Yes, financial data.

Collection

  • How, when, by whom? Loans sent Oct 2021 – Nov 2024. Method and collector unknown.
  • Consent and notice? Unknown. We don't know whether borrowers were told or agreed to this use, or could withdraw. Ghana's Act 843 s.18(2) requires data about foreign people to be handled under their own law, which we can't confirm.
  • Ethical review or impact assessment? Unknown. The BoG Directive requires a DPIA for a licensed lender (para. 2.1(5)(d)).

Preprocessing

  • What we did: a feature audit (learnings/03); removed leaky columns and 190 not-yet-due loans; built borrower history counting a past loan only after its due date (src/features.py).
  • Raw data kept? Yes, in data/, which is never committed.
  • Code: public, in src/ and notebooks/.

Uses

  • Used for: this project's default and amount models, and the fairness audit.
  • Could cause unfair treatment: first-time borrowers and small products are thin in the data, and Kenyan patterns may not apply elsewhere (ADR 0019).
  • Should not be used for: scoring real borrowers without new consent and validation; pricing; identifying anyone; judging groups it doesn't record, like gender.

Distribution

  • Zindi account and competition rules. We redistribute nothing: the repository is public, and only aggregates are published. Raw rows aren't uploaded to AI tools.

Maintenance

  • We don't maintain the source. No known errata process. The competition closed on 12 Jan 2025; late scoring still works. Local copies should be deleted when the course ends.

Part 2: Global Findex Ghana 2021

  • Motivation: made by the World Bank to measure financial inclusion. We use it to describe who lacks credit in Ghana (Part D4).
  • Composition: one row per surveyed adult; 1,000 people, 120 columns, with weights (wgt). Includes gender, age, education, income quintile and rural/urban. No region.
  • Collection: a national survey, 2021. Weighted shares match published figures (68% any account, 60% mobile money, 74.2% of men with an account).
  • Preprocessing: none beyond recoding answers. All results are weighted.
  • Uses: population context only. It can't be linked to borrowers and doesn't test our model.
  • Distribution: World Bank Microdata Library; registration required. We publish aggregates only.
  • Maintenance: by the World Bank. Rural/urban and education codes should be confirmed with the codebook, which we haven't downloaded.

Part 7. Regulatory map and borrower letters

Compliance map and borrower letter

What the design meets and doesn't meet under Ghanaian law. Clause detail and sources are in knowledge/regulation.md. We are students, so this isn't legal advice. We did not read Act 1052, L.I. 2394 or BoG's 2017 disclosure rules, which may add requirements.

Worth noting: the BoG Directive has no rule on algorithms, fairness or automated decisions. Fairness here rests on its consumer-protection goals and on Act 843.

Letter to a declined borrower

The reasons come from src/reasons.py. This applicant is made up.

We could not approve this loan today.

This was an automated decision. The main reasons were: 1. This kind of loan (Type_7) has been repaid less often than others. 2. The loan is for 30 days; longer loans are repaid less often. 3. Your most recent loan (9,000) was larger than your usual loan (3,000).

What you can do - You can ask us to explain or review this decision. Reply HELP. We will answer within 20 days. - You can ask to see the information we hold about you, and to correct it. - You may complain to the Bank of Ghana.

This decision doesn't stop you from applying again.

And for a first-time applicant, who gets a smaller loan rather than a no:

We can offer you a starter loan of GHS 300 today.

This was an automated decision. This is your first loan with us, so we have no borrowing history to go on. Repaying on time helps us offer you more.

Before you accept, we will show you the amount, the due date and the total you must repay. You can ask us to explain or review this decision by replying HELP, and we will answer within 20 days.

And for a Ghanaian applicant asking for a product we aren't offering yet (ADR 0021):

We can't offer this kind of loan (Type_2) in Ghana yet, but you can ask for a starter loan of GHS 300 instead.

This was an automated decision. It is about the product, not about you: about half of loans of this kind have not been repaid in Ghana.

What you can do - Ask for a Type_3 starter loan. We will show you the amount, the due date and the total you must repay before you accept. - Ask us to explain or review this decision. Reply HELP, and we will answer within 20 days. - Ask to see the information we hold about you, and to correct it. You may complain to the Bank of Ghana.

Why the first-time letter is a loan, not a no: our notes warn against "you are a new customer" as the only reason for a decline (Act 726 s.21(1)(p), in spirit). Without the starter loan, that is what first-timers would get. With it, the sentence explains a smaller amount, not a refusal.

Wording rules: no blame; no protected traits; never tell someone to borrow more to qualify; never reveal the model's weights (Act 843 s.35(2) allows this).
